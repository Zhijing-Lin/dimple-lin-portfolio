Building Nutrition Literacy Through Guided Practice
A Storyline e-learning module that uses objective-aligned instruction, interactive practice, feedback, and reflection to help young adults practice making informed food decisions

Image gallery item
Project Overview
I noticed that many college students around me wanted to eat healthier but were unsure where to start or how to translate nutrition information into everyday food choices.

To understand whether this was a broader need, our team conducted a survey with CMU students. The responses confirmed a recurring gap: students were interested in improving their eating habits, but often lacked the practical nutrition knowledge and decision-making skills needed to evaluate food options confidently.

This need became the starting point for the project: designing a learning experience that helps college students move from wanting to eat healthier to making informed, evidence-based nutrition decisions.

Try out the course
Problem
Learners often recognized basic terms such as calories, protein, and carbohydrates, but struggled to apply that knowledge when reading food labels, comparing options, and evaluating balanced choices.

Solution
We designed a practice-based Storyline learning experience that progresses from foundational nutrition concepts to realistic decision-making tasks.

Learners interpret nutrition information, compare food choices, and ultimately evaluate diet plans for a realistic persona using evidence to justify their decisions.

Design Approach
I used a research-based e-learning design approach combining backward design, cognitive task analysis, instructional principles, and learner testing.

We first identified the learning need and target performance, then designed aligned assessment tasks. Next, we used CTA to compare how experts and learners approached a key nutrition decision-making task, revealing reasoning gaps that informed the design.

I translated these findings into the Storyline course structure, practice activities, feedback, reflection prompts, and scaffolding.

Design Alignment
Using backward design, we first identified three target skills:

Recognize and differentiate key nutrients

Interpret Nutrition Facts labels

Apply nutrition knowledge to realistic food decisions

We then designed aligned assessments and practice activities before developing instruction.

Each lesson followed a consistent sequence:

Concept → Practice → Feedback → Reflection

Foundational knowledge was assessed through matching, multiple-choice, and true/false activities, while applied reasoning was assessed through label-reading and persona-based diet-plan decisions.

Cognitive Task Analysis
To understand the reasoning behind successful nutrition decisions, we conducted cognitive task analysis around a key performance task:

Evaluate a persona’s needs, compare diet plans, and justify the best option.

We first mapped an ideal reasoning process, then conducted think-aloud sessions with novice, intermediate, and expert participants.

The analysis revealed a clear difference:

Novices often focused on isolated numbers or surface-level cues.

Experts integrated calorie needs, nutrient priorities, health conditions, food quality, and trade-offs.

This revealed the key learning gap: learners did not only need more nutrition information—they needed a structured way to connect information to decisions.

These findings directly informed the course’s practice, feedback, reflection, and scaffolding.

Instructional Strategy & Storyline Development
I translated the learning goals and CTA findings into an interactive Storyline module that helps learners move from foundational nutrition knowledge to applied decision-making.

The instructional design was guided by:

Procedural scaffolding to support step-by-step reasoning

Metacognitive Reflection to help learners pause, summarize, and check their understanding during the module.

Immediate feedback to correct misconceptions during practice

Personalization to make the learning experience feel relevant and conversational

Multimedia, modality, embodiment, contiguity, and coherence principles to reduce cognitive load and connect visual explanations with narration, character guidance, and focused screen layouts.

Universal Design for Learning and accessibility practices to support different learner needs, preferences, and ways of accessing content

Storyline Development

I independently developed the module in Storyline using:

Custom interactions with layers, states, drag-and-drop, and feedback

Text-entry reflection and completion logic

Learner-controlled navigation and progress support

Accessibility features, including captions, transcripts, alt text, and replay controls

I also created a consistent visual style using simple food and health visuals, soft colors, and clean layouts to keep the experience practical and approachable.


Metacognitive Reflection & Self-Explanation
I embedded reflection prompts throughout the module to encourage learners to explain key concepts in their own words.

After submitting a response, learners could compare their thinking with an example response, helping them identify gaps and refine their reasoning.

This moved reflection beyond passive completion and encouraged learners to actively monitor what they understood.


Procedural Scaffolding & Segmenting
CTA showed that novices often reached a reasonable answer but struggled to explain why the evidence supported their decision.

To address this, I used procedural scaffolding to break a complex diet-plan decision into five manageable steps:

Calorie Fit

Protein Fit

Health Concern

Food Quality

Final Synthesis

Each step provided a focused prompt, relevant reference data, and a sentence starter.

Instead of asking:

“Which plan is best? Explain.”

learners were guided through a structured reasoning process that helped them connect nutrition data to the persona’s specific goals.

This scaffold was intentionally designed to make expert reasoning visible and teachable.

Multimedia, Modality, Embodiment, Contiguity & Coherence
I applied the Multimedia Principle by pairing concise explanations with visuals, examples, tables, and scenario-based screens to make abstract nutrition concepts more concrete.

I used modality and embodiment through narration and a character guide, reducing on-screen text and making the experience feel more guided and conversational.

I applied contiguity and coherence by placing related text and visuals together and keeping each screen focused on one key idea to reduce cognitive load.


Universal Design for Learning & Accessibility
I applied Universal Design for Learning principles by providing multiple ways to access, process, and review content, including:

Visual and written explanations

Narration

Closed captions for audio and video

Transcripts through the Notes panel

Alt text for visual elements

Replay controls

Progress indicators

Learner-controlled navigation

These features support assistive technology use, different content preferences, and greater learner control over pacing and review.

Evaluation
We evaluated the lesson with a pre-test and post-test completed by 20 target learners. The assessment included multiple-choice questions and one open-ended meal plan justification task.

Learners’ average performance increased by approximately 95% after the lesson. Their post-test responses showed stronger evidence-based reasoning, with more attention to calories, protein, calcium, food quality, and the persona’s specific needs.
