AI Architecture Decision-Making Simulation
A multi-modal learning experience combining video and simulation to help AI product managers evaluate AI systems.

Project Overview
I used an AI-augmented research approach to investigate how aspiring AI product managers learn to make AI architecture decisions.

I combined AI-assisted web research and synthetic users to explore recurring questions, misconceptions, and decision-making challenges around AI system design. Rather than treating AI-generated findings as evidence on their own, I used them to form initial hypotheses and then validated those patterns through interviews with CMU students studying product management.

Across these sources, a consistent need emerged: learners were familiar with concepts such as RAG, fine-tuning, and agentic workflows, but lacked a structured way to translate that knowledge into real product decisions.

Try out the course
Problem
The core gap was not conceptual knowledge, but applied decision-making.

When an AI system underperformed, aspiring AI PMs struggled to systematically diagnose the root cause, interpret evaluation results, compare possible architecture changes, and justify a recommendation while balancing performance, cost, and business constraints.

Without a repeatable decision process, these choices could rely too heavily on intuition or isolated metrics rather than evidence.

Solution
To address this gap, we designed a scenario-based learning experience centered on the EVAL framework to give learners structured practice making AI architecture decisions.

Learners work through a realistic AI product scenario in which they evaluate system performance, identify failure modes, compare architecture options, analyze trade-offs, and make a defensible recommendation based on evidence.

Action Mapping
I started with Action Mapping to translate the research findings into observable workplace behaviors.

The performance goal was for learners to make a defensible AI architecture recommendation for an EdTech product by using evaluation results and cost analysis to diagnose failures, compare options, and balance performance, cost, and business constraints.

From this goal, I identified four key actions:

Define success by establishing evaluation criteria and representative test cases

Diagnose failure modes using evaluation results

Compare architecture options using performance and AITCO-based cost data

Recommend and justify a solution based on performance, cost, and product trade-offs

Identifying Learner Barriers
Our research surfaced three key barriers that made AI architecture decision-making difficult for novice product managers:

Defining what “good” looks like
Learners often lacked clear evaluation criteria for judging AI system performance, making it easy to rely on subjective impressions instead of measurable evidence.

Diagnosing why a system is failing
When performance was poor, learners could identify that something was wrong but struggled to determine the underlying failure mode before jumping to a technical solution.

Choosing and justifying the right intervention
Learners found it difficult to compare options such as RAG, fine-tuning, and agentic workflows while weighing performance improvements against cost, complexity, and product constraints.

Practice Activity Design
Using these key actions and learner barriers, I designed four scenario-based tasks focused on applied decision-making rather than concept recall:

Define success: Learners build a rubric for evaluating AI tutor responses, turning an abstract quality like “good tutoring” into measurable criteria.

Diagnose failure modes: Learners analyze AI tutor outputs and identify issues such as answer leaking, logic errors, or instruction-following problems.

Compare architecture options: Learners review evaluation and cost data to decide whether a higher-performing architecture is worth the additional cost.

Justify the recommendation: Learners explain their final decision using evidence from performance metrics, failure analysis, and cost trade-offs.

Storyboarding, Prototyping, and Learner Testing
I first created a storyboard and flow chart to map the full scenario-based learning experience, including the storyline, decision points, feedback moments, and how learners would apply the EVAL framework.

Following the Successive Approximation Model (SAM), I then built an early prototype in Articulate Storyline so we could test the experience before investing in full development.

We conducted three rounds of think-aloud testing with CMU students studying product management, our target learner group. Participants worked through the prototype while explaining their reasoning, confusion points, and decision-making process. After each round, we iterated on the experience based on where learners struggled, what concepts required additional scaffolding, and which interactions or feedback needed clarification.

Pre-training Video

Iteration: Adding a Pre-training Video Before Practice
During think-aloud testing, CMU product management students could often understand individual steps of the EVAL framework, but struggled to see how those steps connected into a complete decision-making process.

To address this gap, we added a pre-training video before the simulation. Created in Synthesia by our multimedia designer, the video introduces the architecture options and provides a high-level mental model of the EVAL process before learners begin making decisions.

This reduced the amount of new information learners had to process during the simulation and allowed them to focus more on applying the framework rather than figuring out its structure.

Evaluation
To evaluate the effectiveness of the learning experience, we conducted a learner survey and pre/post assessment focused on confidence, decision quality, and application of the EVAL framework.

SURVEY

The survey was designed to measure learners’ self-reported confidence in making AI architecture decisions before and after the module.

Example survey items included:

I feel confident distinguishing between RAG, Fine-Tuning, and Agentic Workflows.

I feel confident using evaluation data to diagnose AI system failures.

I feel confident making architecture decisions based on quality, cost, and business constraints.

For a small pilot, the target outcome is to see an increase in learner confidence from approximately 2.5/5 before the module to 4.0/5 after the module.

Learning

We also designed a pre/post assessment to evaluate whether learners could apply the EVAL framework in a realistic product scenario.

Before the module, learners make an architecture decision based on their current reasoning. After completing the video and simulation, they complete a similar scenario and justify their decision using failure diagnosis, architecture comparison, and AITCO-based cost analysis.

The target learning outcome is a measurable improvement in decision quality, from approximately 60% on the pre-assessment to 80% on the post-assessment
